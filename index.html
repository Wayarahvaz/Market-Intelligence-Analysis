03 — CASE STUDY

90-Day Sale & Market Intelligence Report

The business problem: An Energy solution supplier to national power transmission sector was tracking at 18.9% of its annual revenue target with no clear answer for why. Sales data lived in three disconnected places — a legacy spreadsheet, the CRM, and the inbound commercial mailbox — so leadership couldn't tell whether the gap was a demand problem, a conversion problem, or something happening after a deal was already quoted. This report pulls those three sources into one 90-day analysis to answer that question directly: only 2% of inbound e-mails ever convert to closed revenue, but the pipeline already open is more than large enough to close the gap — the real constraint is that 72% of lost deals fail for reasons inside the company's own control (pricing and delivery timelines, not client decisions), and 88% of closed revenue sits in a single client and two service lines. The report turns that diagnosis into an operating-model fix — named account ownership, a real-time CRM as single source of truth, cascading sub-targets, and a standing hand-off from Market Intelligence to Sales on which regulated auctions and sector investments to chase next — closing the loop between "here's what's wrong" and "here's what to do about it."

Methodology: To develop this document, a data set well-built enough to describe sales tendency and behavior was necessary. This data originates from three sources: the Company's project-management spreadsheet, its CRM, and the commercial mailbox that receives inbound requests for quotation. Even with a solid base, a date filter was applied to develop an analysis based on numbers instead of impressions. The window is the last 90 days. This means the analysis does not aim to replace the sales methodology already in place; instead, it establishes a statistical process to prevent an annual revenue gap and a lack of visibility for the sales team. This document aims to fill the gap between the numbers and the overwhelming feeling of lacking strategy, raising the following questions: How are sales going? How are demand requests trending? How much did we sell? How much will we sell at the current pace? What is the current sales scenario? What are the prospects for next year? Which companies can we target? Who are our main clients? How much did we lose, and why? How do we avoid losses? What are the risks?
Conclusion: Based on 90 days of analysis, the Company closed $ 6.05 million against a $ 32 million annual target (18.9%) , a pace that — if unchanged — would close the year at 27% of target ($ 8.64 million in new contracts). Demand itself is not the constraint: 310 commercial e-mails converted to only 38 CRM opportunities and 7 closed deals, a roughly 2.3% inbound win rate, while the qualified pipeline of $ 57.8 million across 14 deals is more than enough to close the gap if conversion improves. The current scenario is one of concentration rather than scarcity: A alone accounts for 79.81% of closed revenue, and Onsite Services together with Equipament supply for 88% of it, so growth depends on diversifying by client and by scope rather than generating more leads. Of the $ 4.94 million lost in the same window, 72% ($ 3.5 million) traces to commercial terms and delivery timelines inside the Company's own control rather than client decisions, which is why avoiding these losses starts internally — with the account-ownership, CRM, and target-cascade changes recommended above — not with a market fix. On the market side, the same answer extends into next year: the named sector signals above and the 2026–2027 auction cycle (a record 6,091 projects registered for the first battery auction, over 6 GW of battery demand projected by 2035, and an approximately $ 2.5 billion transmission auction including the country's first BESS transmission lot) give the Company a dated, external reason to pursue the diversification recommended in this report — both by account and by scope. Read together, the target is not out of reach; closing the gap depends less on generating additional demand and more on commercial alignment. Internal approval cycles that run longer than the client's own deadline are themselves a source of lost deals, which makes a faster, better-prepared path for commercial and technical proposals on priority accounts a priority. Account diversification matters, but only if it comes with the technical and commercial structure to support it — dedicated, qualified sales staff who build client trust from the first proposal, rather than repeated rework caused by under-prepared bids. Shortening that revision-and-rework cycle is what frees the sales team to pursue better, more strategic opportunities, opening a new portfolio of accounts that will demand its own focused, dedicated sales attention.
