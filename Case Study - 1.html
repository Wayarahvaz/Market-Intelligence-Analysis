MODULE 01

Pipeline forecasting & gap-to-target

The starting question wasn't "are we going to hit the number" — it was "what does the pipeline have to look like, by when, for that to even be mathematically possible."

The commercial team tracked bookings, but nobody had modeled the sales-cycle-adjusted version of the forecast. Using 120 days of historical deal data, I calculated an average close cycle of 268 days and used it to back into a hard cutoff: any new opportunity entering the pipeline after that date had no realistic path to closing within the fiscal year.

I built two scenarios side by side — a trend-only projection assuming zero new deals, and a weighted pipeline forecast based on stage-adjusted probabilities. The gap between the two became the number leadership actually needed: how much new, qualified pipeline had to be created, not just chased.

Key insight A closer look at open opportunities also surfaced two high-probability deals (80% close confidence) that had gone 15+ days without follow-up — together worth roughly $1.1M. Flagging stalled-but-winnable deals turned out to move the forecast more than sourcing net-new pipeline.

MODULE 02

Root-cause diagnostic of the sales backlog

A slow pipeline isn't one problem — it's usually several small ones compounding. I mapped 120 days of stage-transition data back to six structural causes rather than treating "slow sales" as the diagnosis itself.

01

Communication breakdowns

Handoffs between sales, application engineering and delivery lost context, adding rework cycles to active deals.

02

Task centralization

Approvals and technical scoping routed through one or two people, creating queueing delays independent of deal complexity.

03

Limited rep autonomy

Reps lacked authority to move standard deals forward without escalation, even on low-risk terms.

04

Undefined process

Stage definitions were inconsistently applied, making the CRM's own stage data an unreliable forecasting input.

05

Resourcing constraints

Application engineering capacity was concentrated on one flagship program, starving the rest of the pipeline of technical support.

06

Fragmented governance

No single owner tracked account-level health across the full deal lifecycle, so stalled deals went unnoticed.

Recommendation Rather than a blanket "sell more" push, the fix was process simplification and clearer account ownership — the backlog was a workflow problem wearing a forecasting costume.

SUMMARY

What this case demonstrates

With current bookings trending at a 279-day average deal cycle, the business was on pace to close the year at roughly 27% of its annual revenue target a gap of over BRL 22.5M that no amount of pipeline chasing alone could close without a change in approach. The forecast model set a hard cutoff date for new opportunities to realistically convert within the fiscal year, and flagged two overlooked deals, each with an 80% close probability, that had sat over 15 days without follow-up, together representing a faster path to target than sourcing entirely new pipeline. A parallel root-cause review of 120 days of deal-stage data traced the backlog to six structural issues, fragmented communication, centralized approvals, limited rep autonomy, undefined process, resourcing concentrated on a single flagship program, and the absence of unified account ownership, reframing what looked like a sales-volume problem as, in large part, a workflow and capacity-allocation one.

As a result, the recommendation split into two tracks: a focused risk review and upsell push on the two high-probability deals, paired with renewed follow-up and margin correction on the lower-probability ones still sitting in pipeline. That, combined with a low-maintenance retention strategy built around existing accounts already in the portfolio, freed the commercial team to pursue higher-margin technical opportunities in adjacent markets segments the company wasn't yet present in, but where growth investment and sector momentum pointed to stronger odds of contract success.
